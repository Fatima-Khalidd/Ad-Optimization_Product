# Getting the first 3 paying clients

A plan for one person, working online, about 6 hours a week, with no budget and
no client list. The goal is **3 paying pilots in 60 days**, not growth.

Everything here assumes what you actually have: warm access through family and
family friends in business, and through university seniors and alumni — plus a
finished product and an account that can receive money.

---

## 0. Fix these before you send the link to anyone

The link is the first thing a prospect judges. Right now it fails on the exact
dimension you are selling: trustworthiness. Four things, roughly a day's work.

1. **Sign-up on the live site is broken.** The website is deployed; the backend
   is not. A prospect who clicks "Create an account" gets an error. Either
   deploy the backend (Supabase + Railway, per the README) **or** — better for
   now — change the landing page buttons to "Get a free audit" pointing at a
   WhatsApp link or a form. You do not want self-serve sign-ups yet anyway. The
   first clients should come through you, personally.
2. **The legal pages say `TODO-OWNER` in 16 places.** Terms, Privacy and Refunds
   are linked in the footer of every page. A careful business owner — exactly
   your buyer — will click them. Write them properly. Plain, short and honest
   beats lawyerly.
3. **Decide your two numbers.** You cannot sell without them: the monthly base
   fee, and the percentage of recovered waste. See §5.
4. **Produce one sample report PDF.** Run the synthetic data, export the PDF,
   and write "SAMPLE — synthetic data" on the first page. This is your single
   most valuable sales asset: it lets someone see the output before handing you
   anything. Never imply it is a real client's account.

## 1. Who you are actually selling to

Three groups. The third is not a customer — it is a multiplier, and for your
constraints it may matter most.

### Persona A — The D2C brand owner

Clothing, unstitched fabric, skincare, supplements, home goods. Sells through
Instagram DMs, a Shopify or WooCommerce store, and sometimes Daraz. Spends
Rs. 150,000–400,000 a month, heavily concentrated around Eid, Ramzan and 11.11.
Either runs ads personally or has one in-house person doing it.

- **What they actually care about:** cost per order and how many orders today.
  Not CTR, not reach, not "engagement".
- **What hurts:** they can see total spend and total orders, but not which slice
  of the spend produced them. They suspect money is leaking, and cannot prove it.
- **The line that lands:** *"You know your cost per order overall. This tells you
  your cost per order for each placement, age group and time of day — so you can
  see which ones are dragging the average up."*
- **Where they are:** Instagram, e-commerce Facebook groups, Daraz seller groups.
- **Seasonal timing matters:** approach them **before** a big season, when budget
  is about to go up, not during it when nobody has time to talk.

### Persona B — The local service business

Dental and skin clinics, salons, gyms, schools and academies, real estate
agents, travel agents, car rentals. Spends Rs. 100,000–250,000 a month on
lead-generation ads. Usually the owner approves the budget, and a junior person
or a freelancer runs the ads.

- **What they actually care about:** cost per lead, and how many leads were junk.
- **What hurts:** they pay for leads that never answer the phone, and they
  suspect certain audiences or times of day produce nothing but noise.
- **The line that lands:** *"Your overnight ads cost the same as your midday ads.
  If they convert at a fraction of the rate, you are paying full price for your
  worst hours."* Time-of-day waste is very visible to this group and easy for
  them to act on.
- **Where they are:** LinkedIn (clinics, real estate, education), local business
  Facebook groups, and — most reliably for you — family friends.

### Persona C — The freelancer or one-person agency (your multiplier)

Runs ads for 5–15 clients at Rs. 30,000–60,000 a month per client. Your
university seniors will know several. **Do not sell to them as a customer — sell
to them as a tool that makes them look good.**

- **What they actually care about:** keeping clients, and having something
  concrete to show at the monthly review.
- **The line that lands:** *"Bring this to your client meeting. It makes you
  look rigorous, and it is evidence you are actively managing their money."*
- **Why this matters for you:** one freelancer can introduce you to several
  advertisers at once. Even one good relationship here can produce your first
  three pilots by itself.
- **The obvious tension:** if the report says the freelancer wasted money, they
  look bad. Handle it by framing the report as *their* finding, not your
  verdict on them, and by offering a white-label option later.

**Who to ignore:** anyone spending under Rs. 50,000 a month (too little data,
too little saving to justify a fee) and anyone with a real marketing team (they
have tools, and you cannot compete on features yet).

## 2. The four messages

Built on your positioning. Written for someone who has been disappointed by an
agency before and is now suspicious of confident claims.

### Message 1 — "You can check every number yourself"

> It is arithmetic, not AI. We take the rows you gave us, add up the spend and
> the conversions for each segment, divide one by the other, and compare. You
> can redo it in Excel. If your numbers disagree with ours, we are wrong and we
> will show our working.

**Why this works:** every competitor says "AI-powered". Refusing that word is
the most memorable thing you can say, and it's *falsifiable* — which is the
point. Say it early and let people test it.

### Message 2 — "We compare you to you"

> No industry benchmarks. Nobody can verify what "the average clothing brand in
> Pakistan" spends per order, and anyone quoting it is guessing. Your account is
> measured against your own average, from your own data.

**Why this works:** Pakistani SMB owners have repeatedly been shown imported
benchmarks that mean nothing locally. This says you know that.

### Message 3 — "We never ask for access to your ad account"

> Export a CSV and send it. No passwords, no Business Manager access, no
> agency taking over your account. If you want, blank out the campaign names
> first — the analysis does not need them.

**Why this works:** losing control of an ad account is a genuine, common fear,
and the offer to anonymise campaign names costs you nothing and proves you
understood the concern.

### Message 4 — "Here is what we refuse to do"

> The same wasted rupee shows up in the placement view, the age view and the
> time-of-day view. Adding them together would let us claim three times the
> waste — and bill you for it. We report the largest single view instead.
> Segments too small to be meaningful are shown to you but never flagged.

**Why this works:** this is the strongest thing you have. Anyone can claim
accuracy; describing a specific way you *could* have inflated your own invoice
and chose not to is proof of character. It also directly pre-empts "you are
just trying to make the number look big".

Use messages 1 and 3 to open. Save message 4 for the moment someone gets
sceptical — it turns their scepticism into trust.

**One rule:** never promise a percentage saving. You do not know their account.
"I do not know yet, that is what the audit is for" is more persuasive than a
number you invented, to this audience especially.

## 3. Channels, ranked for your constraints

Online only, ~6 hours a week, warm network available. Ranked by return per hour.

### 1. Warm introductions — do this first, it is most of your chance

Your family, family friends, and university seniors and alumni. Not because
they will buy, but because **they know someone who advertises**.

- **Effort:** low. **Likely return:** high. This is where first clients come from.
- **The ask is an introduction, not a sale.** "Do you know anyone who spends
  serious money on Facebook ads? I built something and I want to test it on a
  real account for free."
- Being a student is an **advantage** here, not a liability. People help
  students. They do not help vendors. Say you are building your first product
  and you need real data to test against — it is true, and it lowers their risk.
- Target: 25–30 people asked in the first two weeks.

### 2. Freelancers and small agencies through university seniors

- **Effort:** low–medium. **Likely return:** high, and compounding.
- One conversation can lead to several advertisers.
- Approach: "I built a tool that audits ad accounts for waste. Can I run it on
  one of your accounts for free? If it finds nothing, that is useful to you too."

### 3. Meta Ad Library — for targeted cold outreach

This is the highest-quality cold list available to you, and it is free.
[facebook.com/ads/library](https://www.facebook.com/ads/library/) shows every ad
currently running. Filter to Pakistan, pick a category, and you can see which
businesses are running **many ads right now** — that is, actively spending.

- **Effort:** medium. **Likely return:** medium.
- A business running 15+ active ads is spending real money. That is your list.
- Reach them by Instagram DM or the WhatsApp number on their page. Reference a
  specific ad you saw. Generic DMs get ignored; "I saw you're running the winter
  collection ads" does not.
- Realistic response rate: 5–15%. Budget 20 DMs a week, not 200.

### 4. Facebook and WhatsApp business groups

Pakistan e-commerce communities, Daraz seller groups, Shopify Pakistan groups,
digital marketing groups.

- **Effort:** medium. **Likely return:** medium, but slow — and easy to get
  wrong.
- **Do not post promotions.** You will be removed, and worse, remembered.
- Instead: answer ad questions helpfully for 2–3 weeks with no pitch at all.
  People will look at your profile. Make sure your profile says what you do.
  One genuinely useful answer about cost-per-result beats ten promotional posts.

### 5. LinkedIn

- **Effort:** medium. **Likely return:** low–medium now, higher later.
- Best use: reaching Persona B (clinics, real estate, education) and agency
  people, plus building a credible profile that people check after you DM them.
- Post occasionally about what you are building and what you find. Not a content
  calendar — just proof you exist and think clearly.

### 6. Cold email — **skip it.** Low open rates, no warm signal, high effort.

### 7. Paid ads — **skip it entirely at this stage.** See §6.

## 4. The first 30 days

About 6 hours a week — roughly an hour a day, with a longer session at the
weekend. Total ≈ 26 hours. Be honest with yourself about the hours; a plan you
half-do is worse than a smaller plan you finish.

### Week 0 (the weekend before) — 4 hours

- Fix the sign-up button, or deploy the backend (§0.1).
- Write the three legal pages properly (§0.2).
- Decide your base fee and percentage (§5).
- Generate the sample PDF and mark it SAMPLE.
- Write your one-paragraph explanation and practise saying it out loud. If you
  cannot explain it in 30 seconds, nobody will listen to the other 5 minutes.

**Done when:** you can send someone the link and the sample PDF without wincing.

### Week 1 — build the list and ask (6 hours)

- **Write down 30 names.** Family, family friends, seniors, alumni, anyone in
  business. Put them in a spreadsheet with a column for "asked" and "replied".
- **Message 15 of them.** Individually. Not a broadcast — a broadcast is obvious
  and people resent it.
- **Template:**
  > Assalam-o-alaikum [name], hope you're well. I've built a tool that looks at
  > a business's Facebook/Google ad data and finds which parts of the budget
  > aren't producing sales. I'm looking for 2–3 businesses to try it on for
  > free, so I can test it on real data. Do you know anyone spending Rs. 1 lakh
  > or more a month on ads? Happy to show you what the output looks like.
- Set up a folder structure for client files, and write a one-page data
  agreement (§5).

**Target:** 15 asked, 3–5 replies, 1–2 introductions.

### Week 2 — run the first free audits (7 hours)

- Message the remaining 15 warm contacts.
- Message 5 freelancers or agency contacts through seniors.
- **Deliver your first audit.** The process:
  1. They send the CSV (tell them exactly which export and which columns — write
     this down once and reuse it).
  2. You run it. If the file doesn't parse, **fix it yourself**, do not send them
     back error messages. Real exports are messy; this is the point at which you
     find out how messy.
  3. You send the PDF **and get on a call or a voice note to walk them through
     it.** Do not just email the file. The conversation is where trust forms and
     where you learn what confuses people.
- **After each audit, write down:** what they asked, what they misunderstood,
  and whether they believed the number.

**Target:** 2–3 audits delivered.

### Week 3 — convert (7 hours)

- Follow up on every audit within 3 days. The single most common reason a
  founder loses a deal is not following up.
- **The ask:** *"The audit found Rs. X of waste. If you make these changes, I'll
  re-run it next month and we can see whether it actually came back. That
  monthly service is Rs. [base fee] plus [Y]% of whatever we confirm you
  recovered. If nothing is recovered, you pay only the base fee."*
- Expect to be told "let me think about it". Ask what specifically they want to
  think about; the answer is usually an objection they haven't said out loud.
- Keep the warm asks going — 10 more.

**Target:** 1–2 saying yes.

### Week 4 — prove it and compound (6 hours)

- Write your first case study. One page: their situation, what you found, what
  they changed, what happened. **Ask permission**, and anonymise if they prefer
  ("a Lahore clothing brand spending Rs. 300k/month").
- **Ask every audited business for one introduction**, whether or not they paid.
  This is the highest-return hour of the month.
- Post the case study on LinkedIn, with real numbers if allowed.
- Review what you learned: which persona responded, which message landed, how
  long an audit really took.

**Target:** 2–3 paying pilots, 1 case study, 3+ referrals.

### The funnel, realistically

To land 2–3 pilots, expect roughly: **40–50 approaches → 10–12 real
conversations → 5–6 free audits → 2–3 paying**. If you get far fewer audits than
that, the problem is your opening message. If you get audits but no conversions,
the problem is either your price or your follow-up.

## 5. Pricing, and the paperwork you need

**A concrete starting point** — your call, but do not launch without deciding:

- **Base fee:** Rs. 10,000–15,000/month. High enough to be taken seriously, low
  enough to be a rounding error against a Rs. 200,000 ad budget.
- **Share of recovered waste:** 20%. Simple to explain, and familiar.
- **For the first three pilots:** waive the base fee for the first month. Charge
  the performance share only. You want the case study more than the money, and
  "you pay nothing unless it works" removes most of the risk for a stranger.

**Write a one-page data agreement.** Half a page is fine. It should say: what
you receive (ad performance figures, no customer data), what you do with it,
that you will not share it, how long you keep it, and that you will delete it on
request. Send it unprompted, before they ask. For a student asking a business
for their data, volunteering this is worth more than anything you could say.

## 6. What not to do

1. **No paid ads.** You have no proof, no case study and no budget. Money spent
   here buys you nothing but a lesson you can get free.
2. **No content calendar.** Not until you have a client. You have 6 hours a
   week; spending them writing posts nobody reads instead of talking to people
   is the most common way solo founders avoid the scary part.
3. **No logo, brand palette or redesign.** The site is already good enough.
4. **No new features until a real client asks.** You will be tempted to add
   Google Ads support, or a dashboard widget, when outreach gets uncomfortable.
   Building is your comfort zone; recognise the avoidance for what it is.
5. **Do not chase bigger clients.** A Rs. 5 crore-a-month advertiser will want
   things you cannot deliver, and will take three months to say no.
6. **Do not spam groups.** One promotional post in a Pakistani seller group can
   get you known as the person who spams. It is a small community.
7. **Do not say "AI" to sound impressive.** It contradicts your entire
   positioning, and the first technical person who tests it will catch you.
8. **Do not promise a savings percentage.** Especially not before you have run
   the tool on a single real account.
9. **Do not offer the audit free forever.** Free for the first three, then stop.
   A permanent free tier attracts people who will never pay, and you have 6
   hours a week.
10. **Do not hide that you are a student.** It comes out eventually and looks
    like something you were concealing. Said up front, it is charming and
    disarming.

## 7. What to track

One spreadsheet. Five numbers, updated weekly. Nothing else.

| Number | Target by day 30 | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Warm asks sent | 40 | The only input fully in your control |
| Real conversations | 10 | Tests whether your opening message works |
| Free audits delivered | 5 | Tests whether people trust you with data |
| Paying pilots | 2–3 | The only outcome that counts |
| Referrals received | 3 | Predicts whether month 2 is easier than month 1 |

Also record, for your own learning: **hours spent per audit** (if it is 4 hours,
your pricing is wrong) and **rupees of waste found vs. rupees confirmed
recovered** (if found is large but recovered is near zero, clients are not
acting on the reports — which is a product problem, not a marketing one).

**Do not track:** website visits, LinkedIn impressions, followers, sign-ups.
None of them predict revenue at this stage, and watching them feels like
progress while being none.

## 8. Two risks to be honest about

**The tool has never run on a real Meta export.** Every test so far has used
data you generated yourself. Real exports have different column names, missing
rows, changed currencies and odd date formats. Your first free audit is as much
a product test as a sales call — treat a broken import as the expected outcome,
budget time to fix it, and do not let the client see the error.

**An audit may find very little.** Some accounts really are well run. If that
happens, say so plainly: *"I looked, and your spend is fairly even across
segments — there's no big leak here."* You will lose that sale and gain a
reputation, which at this stage is worth more. It also validates the tool: a
tool that finds waste in every account it touches is not measuring anything.
